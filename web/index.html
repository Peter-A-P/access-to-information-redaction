Access to information: rules, then Jev, a decision model, with every number measured

Hundreds of pages, redacted in an afternoon with Jev, and a person still decides every line

When someone asks a public body for its records, staff read every page and decide, line by line, what must be withheld and what must be released. The law turns on context: the requester's own details go back to them, an official signing a letter stays, a neighbour who made a complaint is withheld. Off-the-shelf privacy tools black out every name and number, which answers the request in form and refuses it in substance, so the reading is still done by hand. This tool reads the record the way a reviewer does, proposes every redaction with the section of the law it rests on, and leaves the decision on every page to a person, with a log nobody can quietly edit. What the rules cannot settle is decided by Jev, a new kind of AI model: a decision model, which picks among the categories of the law and says how likely each one is instead of writing an answer, and which took what the tool withholds from the requester from {{rules_over}} with the rules alone to {{full_over}}. Jev never sees a name: every person, contact and identifier on the page is replaced with a placeholder before the question leaves, it goes out only through the portfolio's own AI gateway, and what comes back is probabilities, not text.

Why it is still done by hand

The same kind of detail, a name or a telephone number, is released on one line and withheld on the next, and nothing in the name says which. What decides it is who the person is in this record. That is the judgement privacy tools do not attempt, so they withhold everything, and a public body that used one would be refusing most of what the law says it must release.

One page, in seven steps

The order is the design. The context is built before anything is decided, the rules decide what has a settled answer, and nothing is released until a person has acted on every page.

  1. 1ReadRecover the layout: letterhead, email headers, signature blocks, even from a scan.
  2. 2FindEvery name, number, address and title that could be personal information.
  3. 3PlaceBuild each person's context: their title, their block, every other mention of them.
  4. 4DecideFixed rules in a fixed order, each tied to a section of the law.
  5. 5AskJev, a decision model, on what is left, seeing placeholders, never a name. Unsure, it escalates.
  6. 6ReviewA person approves or overrules every proposal, page by page, all of it logged.
  7. 7ReleaseRemove the text under every box, then test that nothing is left.

Step 6 is highlighted because it is the only one that can release anything. How it's built follows a span through all seven.

Withholding what must go, releasing what must stay

A leak is a third party's information released. Over-redaction is something the requester was entitled to, withheld. A tool can drive the first to zero by withholding everything, and the blunt tool nearly does; the price is the second number, which is the one nobody publishes. Lower and further left is better on both.

The context is the whole argument

A person's capacity is almost never in the sentence that names them. It is three inches lower, in the signature block, or at the top, on the letterhead. So for every span the tool builds a context record: the kind of block it sits in, the heading above it, whether a title is paired with this person anywhere in the record, whether they are on a public register, every other mention of the same person, and whether they are the requester. Blank that record and keep the same rules, and over-redaction more than doubles.

Where every leak comes from

A leak can only happen four ways: the detector never found the span; it found it and called it a place or an organisation, which is not personal information; the box was too short; or the decision was wrong. Counting them separately says which part to fix, and a two-way split once pointed a fix at the wrong module.

The decisions are not where the leaks are. Of the tool's {{full_leaks}} leaks, {{tool_never_found}} were never found, {{tool_mislabelled}} were found and typed as a place or an organisation, {{tool_box_short}} left part of a name showing, and {{tool_decision_wrong}} were a wrong decision, by the rules or by Jev. The weak part is detection, and that is where the work goes.

What that adds up to

Federal institutions processed {{federal_pages}} pages of access-to-information requests in {{federal_pages_year}}, by the Treasury Board's statistical report (Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada excluded, as the report excludes it). At the {{secs_saved}} seconds a page the study measured, that is about {{hours_saved}} hours of review saved a year, or {{people_saved}} people reviewing full time ({{hours_per_year}} hours a year each). At the top-step salary of a PM-04 analyst, the level federal ATIP analysts are most often classified at, ${{analyst_salary}} in the Program and Administrative Services agreement (effective June 2024), that time is worth about ${{dollars_saved}} million a year, before benefits or overhead, so the real cost is higher.

This is arithmetic on a measurement, not a measurement. The seconds come from one-page invented records, and a real page can be denser or emptier; it counts only the reading and deciding, not finding the records, consulting other bodies or writing the response. Read it as the scale of the work the tool is aimed at.

Faster and more accurate, which is the combination that matters. By hand, the misses were the kind people make: a first name standing alone later in the text, the second appearance of a full name, a selection one character short of an email address. With proposals, the misses were the tool's own boxes, approved as drawn. Both kinds are why the screen now withholds every other occurrence of a value the moment a reviewer withholds one.

For the technical reader: what the study can and cannot say
  • {{reviewers_word_cap}} people, the first with eight years in access-to-information work. Every reviewer did the proposals half second, so some of the gain is familiarity with the templates; the interval resamples records, not people.
  • The proposals are frozen as they stood for the first reviewer, so every later reviewer saw the same tool and the arms stay comparable as the tool improves. They were the rules alone: Jev came after the study. The proposals the reviewers timed withheld {{study_tool_over}} of what had to be released on their records; the tool as it ships withholds {{full_over}} across the corpus, so the study measured the weaker tool, and the time with Jev's proposals is not yet measured.
  • The reviewers changed the labels twice, and every table carries both rulings: a family word that points at a third party is withheld with the name, and the parties to a confidential workplace complaint are withheld with their titles. Where a reviewer departed from a label and the label stood, it counts against the reviewer.
  • Seconds are the server's own, from serving a page to its completion row in the log. results/reviewer.md and docs/reviewer-study.md.

A black box is not a redaction

The most common redaction failure in the news is a black rectangle drawn over text that is still in the file, one copy and paste from public. Every export here removes the text under each box, and then a test tries to get it back two ways: from the PDF's text layer, and by rendering the page to an image and reading it with OCR.

Scanned paper

Many records arrive as scans. The same {{scan_documents}} records were printed to PDF, degraded the way a scanner, a photocopier and a fax degrade a page, and read back with Tesseract. What a good scan costs is not the characters; it is the layout. A scan has no signature block in it, only lines of text, so the tool reads the blocks back from the words a signature and an email header are made of.

A scanner costs little; a fax costs the record. With the layout read back and Jev deciding what the rules leave, an office scan withholds {{scan_scanner_over}} of what must be released, against {{scan_native_over}} on the native file and {{scan_scanner_rules_over}} with the rules alone. Reading the layout back is what makes the rules usable on a scan at all: read as plain paragraphs, they withhold {{scan_scanner_flat_over}}. At {{scan_fax_cer}} of characters misread, a fax destroys the values before any detector sees them, and the leak rate is {{scan_fax_leak}}, against {{scan_fax_rules_leak}} for the rules alone: on text that broken Jev's guesses are guesses. Fax-quality records need a person, and the tool's number says so.

Real released records

Invented records have exact labels and template-shaped text. Published federal releases are the opposite: real, untidy, and everything still visible was judged releasable by a person at Library and Archives Canada. So the question asked of them is one-sided but honest: of what a person released, how much would this tool have withheld? {{rel_packages_total}} packages, {{rel_pages_total}} pages of Department of National Defence records from 2001 to 2010, the collection the tool was adapted to.

This is the tool's weakest result, and it is published as such. Military ranks and units, layout read back from the text, and Jev take it from withholding everything to {{rel_defence_now}}, where the rules alone now withhold {{rel_defence_rules}}. These are scanned forms with no layout and no requester, so every effect pushes towards withholding, and the military vocabulary was written after reading these packages, so this is not a blind test.

The tool was also run on {{rel_other_packages}} packages from other collections (External Affairs, Immigration, Health, Justice), chosen by a rule written before any was downloaded, with nothing adapted to them. There it withholds {{rel_heldout_now}} and {{rel_fresh_now}} of what a person released: heads of state it has no register for, and family words in century-old immigration files the archive opened because everyone in them was born more than 110 years ago. Those figures say how much adapting the tool to a collection is worth, not how it does on one it was adapted to, so they are not charted.

For the technical reader: why a leak rate cannot be reported here
  • One-sided by construction. What sat under the original black boxes was removed before publication, so there is no must-withhold denominator. The scoring code raises rather than return a leak rate on this track.
  • A floor, not a forecast. These are OCR text layers of scanned forms with no layout and no configured requester, so every effect pushes towards withholding.
  • The archive's own age rule (release once 110 years have passed since birth, Privacy Regulations s. 6(c)) was built and registered before the Health and Justice packages were opened; no page in it was old enough for the rule to read, so it decided nothing there, and that is reported too.
  • releases.md, releases-heldout.md, releases-fresh.md, and the selection rules in docs/data.md.

Jev, on what the rules leave

The rules decide what has a settled answer. What they leave goes to Jev 1.14, and Jev is not a chatbot asked to classify. It is a decision model: it takes a state and a question with named options and returns a probability for every option, and no text at all. Here the state is the whole page with every name, contact and identifier replaced by a typed placeholder, sent through the portfolio's own AI gateway, and the options are the categories of the law in the reviewers' own words. That is exactly the question this tool asks on every span, and a model built to answer it changed the result more than anything else in the project.

How Jev decides without seeing anyone

  1. FindProject 04's PII engine, with this project's own recognisers, finds every name, contact, identifier, address and date on the page.
  2. MaskEach is replaced with a typed placeholder, such as [PERSON_2] or [PHONE_1], and a second pass masks anything name- or number-shaped the engine missed. Job titles, organisations and family words stay, because the decision turns on them.
  3. CheckThe finished request is searched for every value that was found. If one is still there, the request is refused before anything is sent.
  4. SendIt goes out only through the portfolio's AI gateway (project 04), which records every call as personal data and caps the spend. There is no other route to the model.
  5. AnswerJev returns a probability for each category and no text, so nothing personal comes back and nothing has to be put back. The answer is matched to its span here, where the real values stayed.

Why a decision model, and not a better language model? A language model answers by writing. Ask it for a category and it writes one; ask how sure it is and it writes a number, which is just more text. Claude Haiku 5.5 is a good model, and its number does mean something here. The trouble is which way it goes wrong: some of its mistakes release what should have been withheld. Take every answer it gives and the leak rate rises to {{haiku_leak_accept_all}}, above the {{jev_leak_accept_all}} the rules leak on their own. To stay safe it had to run at a threshold of 0.90, which sent a quarter of its answers to a person, nearly all of them correct.

Jev gets about as many answers right as Haiku on the same random sample of spans. The difference is where its mistakes fall. TypeSafe says Jev's probabilities are calibrated, and here they are. Take every answer Jev gives and the leak rate stays at {{jev_leak_accept_all}}, exactly the rules' own: when Jev is wrong, it withholds something that could have been released, and on this corpus it never released a third party (the trap set found one exception, described below). That is why the threshold can sit low enough to use nearly all of Jev's work, and why the requester gets back what they are entitled to instead of a page of escalations.

It fits the rest of the design too. No prose means nothing to parse and nothing that could restate the record; "cannot tell" is a low probability rather than a refusal, so every span gets a decision and a confidence and the threshold decides what a person sees; it is sent exactly what the language model was sent, through the same guard that refuses any request with a detected value in it; and at {{jev_secs}} seconds a decision, with the rules settling two thirds of the spans first, a page's model time is a few seconds. The clip at the top of this page runs at that pace.

The open-source alternative did not work. Kev is an open-weights recreation of Jev, so it was tested on the same requests, in two sizes. Kev 4B ran over the whole corpus through OpenRouter for {{kev4b_cost}}. It does read the records: it got {{kev4b_sample_right}} of a random sample of {{sample_spans}} spans right, against Jev's {{jev_sample_right}}. But it is never sure of itself. Almost every probability it gave was under 0.70, and its answers were no more often right when it sounded surer, so at Jev's threshold it accepts almost nothing and the tool withholds {{kev4b_over}}, the same as the rules alone; taking all its answers instead adds leaks. Kev 0.8B, run on this laptop, got {{kev08b_sample_right}} right, picking categories by their place in the list rather than by reading them. Whether a shorter question would suit Kev better was not tested.

The portfolio's release gate, asked with the document as the unit, passes it: {{gate_rules}} of documents leak-free with the rules alone, {{gate_model}} with Jev, inside its three-point margin.

For the technical reader: what weakens the result
  • The threshold the tool runs at is not the one the rule picked. The rule fixed before looking, the lowest threshold that adds no leak on the validation split, picked {{thr_picked}}. On the test split that leaks {{thr_test_picked_leak}} and withholds {{thr_test_picked_over}}, against {{thr_test_rules_leak}} and {{thr_test_rules_over}} for the rules alone; that is the reported result. The tool runs at {{model_threshold}}, chosen after the test split was seen so that Jev's coin flips go to a person: {{thr_test_running_over}} there.
  • It leaked on the trap set, and the fix came after. An employee writing from their work address about their own leave was released as an official on 8 of 240 must-withhold spans, at 0.60 to 0.70, where the rules alone and Haiku released none. A release in such a message now needs {{own_affairs_bar}}: the trap set then leaks {{trap_leak}} and withholds {{trap_over}}, against {{trap_rules_over}} for the rules alone. The bar was set on the records it is scored on.
  • Some of what it is told came from this corpus's errors. The category guide was improved by reading Haiku's mistakes here; the rulings existed before, but the corpus that exposed them is the one scored.
  • Without the rules Jev leaks {{model_only_leak}} against {{full_leak}} with them, and makes wrong decisions the rules never make: the requester's own information, a family word, a party to a confidential complaint. The rules also settle two thirds of the spans for nothing.
  • It is not open. TypeSafe does not disclose its size or base model, and it is reached through OpenRouter. Every answer it gave is committed, so every table replays without it; an open-weights recreation run on the same requests spread its probabilities too thin to use.
  • No raw entity leaves. A test asserts that no detected value appears in any outbound request, page included; the gateway records the data class of every call. {{model_asks}} questions were asked for the published tables.

The law, as data

Every proposal names a category, and every category maps to the sections of the legislation it rests on, in two jurisdictions. The mapping is a data file, not code, so a public body can read it, argue with it and change it. Each of the {{provisions}} provisions carries the quoted text, the date it was checked and the address it was checked against, and the file refuses one that has none.

{{taxonomy_rows}}
CategoryCanada (federal)Newfoundland and Labrador

ATIA is the Access to Information Act; ATIPPA is Newfoundland and Labrador's Access to Information and Protection of Privacy Act, 2015. Where the two disagree, as on what is already public, the provincial file escalates rather than releases. The tool decides only personal information: advice, legal privilege, law enforcement and business interests are flagged for the reviewer with the provision and never decided.

What did not work

Looking officials up in the staff directory by name. Most of the over-redaction of officials is a name the record never pairs with a title, and the obvious remedy is to look the name up. Measured, it multiplied the leak rate by {{dir_leak_factor_word}}, from {{dir_leak_none}} to {{dir_leak_names}}. Some of that is the small invented name pool, but {{dir_listed_leaks}} of the leaks were the listed employee themselves: an employee who files a grievance is in the directory and is a third party in their own complaint. A directory is evidence about a string; the decision turns on the sentence. Only the work email address survived, and it is now the only thing a directory is matched on.

What this does not do

It does not release anything

Every page needs a person's explicit action, and the export asks the audit log, which refuses while any page is undecided.

It decides personal information only

Advice, legal privilege, law enforcement and business interests are flagged with the provision and left to the reviewer.

It is not legal advice

The categories map to two statutes and are configurable. A public body applies its own interpretation.

The corpus is invented

Exact labels need people cast before the documents are written. Real releases, above, are where that is checked, and they are its weakest result.

It does not read handwriting

The scanned track covers typed pages. A fax-quality page loses most of its values before detection.

The study is {{reviewers_word}} people

Each did both halves, proposals second, so familiarity is part of the gain. The interval resamples records, not reviewers.
